`material::catalogue` lists materials with everything a design needs about them, each number
traced to the paper it comes from: the refractive index (one or more models, each a `Material`
for given conditions), the crystal, the second-order nonlinear tensor $d_{il}$, the linear
electro-optic tensor $r_{ij}$, and a few constants such as static permittivities. Every value was
read from its primary paper; a property with no primary number in hand is listed as coming, with
the paper it will come from (`Entry::coming`), rather than filled from a compilation. Each entry
also has tags (`Entry::tags`): what the crystal is and what its symmetry allows, each a statement
with its physics, caveats and sources. The studio's Materials page shows it all, the tags and what
is coming as hover cards.

Python has the indices: `materials` lists the catalogue's materials with their index
models, each model's range and sources, and `refractive_index` and `group_index` evaluate one at
vacuum wavelengths, the default model unless `model` names another. The tensors are Rust's only
([features](../features.md)). Silicon's default is Li's (1980) at 293 K, his Table 1 at 1.55 µm:

```python
import photonoxide as phox

si = next(m for m in phox.materials() if m.id == "si")
default = next(model for model in si.models if model.default)
lo, hi = default.range_um                            # where it is valid; outside, an error
n = phox.refractive_index("si", wavelength_um=1.55)   # n + iκ, a complex number for one wavelength
assert round(n.real, 4) == 3.4757                    # Li 1980, Table 1, 293 K
ng = phox.group_index("si", wavelength_um=[1.5, 1.55, 1.6])
assert ng.shape == (3,) and all(ng > n.real)         # normal dispersion: n_g above n
```

## Thermal conductivities

Beside the indices, `catalogue::conductivities()` has each material's thermal conductivity
(`Entry::conductivity`, or `catalogue::conductivity("Si")` by id or formula), every model with its
paper, temperature range, stated accuracy and, for a film, thickness; `ConductivityModel::
conductivity(T, d)` refuses a value outside them. The static solver's heat jobs take them for the
media a job gives no value ([static](static.md#as-a-job)).

| Material | Model | Range | At 300 K, W/(m·K) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Si, bulk (default) | recommended values | 200–1000 K (±5 % from 300 K; below, typical values) | 148 | Ho, Powell & Liley 1972, p. 394 |
| Si, bulk | $1/\kappa = 1.60 \times 10^{-5} T + 2.37 \times 10^{-8} T^2$ | 220–320 K | 144.2 | Kremer et al. 2004, natural Si |
| Si, bulk | measured single crystals | 50–1681 K (±5 %) | 156 | Glassbrenner & Slack 1964, Table I |
| Si, film | Sondheimer's reduction, $\Lambda = 0.3$ µm, on the bulk's 148 | 300 K; 74 nm–10 µm | 90.7 at 220 nm | Ju & Goodson 1999 |
| SiO₂ | recommended values, clear fused quartz | 100–900 K (±4 % to 500 K) | 1.38 | Powell, Ho & Liley 1966, Table 14 |
| W | recommended values | 200–1000 K (±3 %) | 174 | Ho et al. 1972, p. 408 |
| Ti | recommended values, polycrystalline | 200–1000 K (±10 %) | 21.9 | Ho et al. 1972, p. 407 |
| Pt | recommended values | 200–1000 K (±3 %) | 71.6 | Ho et al. 1972, p. 379 |

- **Tables.** Between the rows at whole hundreds of kelvin (and 250 K) $k$ is a power law of $T$,
  $k_i (T/T_i)^{\ln(k_{i+1}/k_i)/\ln(T_{i+1}/T_i)}$; the tables' rows between them (223.2 …
  973.2 K) check it. The metals' are the pure, annealed metals': a deposited film, with its grain
  boundaries and impurities, conducts less.
- **Films.** A monocrystalline film conducts less along it than bulk, its phonons scattered at
  its surfaces. Ju & Goodson measured SOI films of 74 to 240 nm at 300 K and found the effective
  mean free path of the phonons that carry the heat near 0.3 µm: the thickness at which
  Sondheimer's reduction for diffuse surfaces, $1 - \frac{3}{2\delta}\int_1^\infty (t^{-3} -
  t^{-5})(1 - e^{-\delta t})\,dt$ with $\delta = d/\Lambda$, matches their data. In closed form it
  is $1 - \frac{3}{2\delta}(\frac14 - E_3(\delta) + E_5(\delta))$, the $E_n$ exponential
  integrals ($E_1$ by its series below 1 and a continued fraction above, the rest by
  recurrence). The model gives the conductivity along the film and takes it for across too, where
  it is lower still; at 300 K only, as the paper measured.
- **Two bulk silicons over the whole range.** Glassbrenner & Slack measured high-purity single
  crystals from 3 K to the melting point, in two apparatus neither adjusted to the other (±5 %,
  ±2 % between two points on one sample); their Table I gives silicon from 50 to 1681 K (below,
  its K depends on the sample's size). They lie 5.4 to 6.2 % above Ho et al.'s recommended values
  from 300 to 400 K, within the two papers' ±5 % each, and within 1 % at 900 and 1000 K; above
  1000 K, where Ho et al.'s table stops, electrons and holes carry part of the heat, 40 % at the
  melting point. Ho et al.'s stays the default; Glassbrenner & Slack's reaches cryogenic heaters
  and anneals.
- **Not here yet.** TiN, whose films' conductivity depends on how they are made (Jacques et al.'s
  Table 3 takes 28 W/(m·K) from a handbook): a heater job gives it.

- **Lithium niobate.** M. Lee measured congruent crystals, poled along z, by the phase and
  magnitude of their reflection in a quasi-optical bridge from 90 to 147 GHz, the field along y
  or z, averaging 20 spectra; the paper states no temperature. It prints no table: the catalogue
  reads Fig. 2's 29 points per curve (every 2 GHz from 91 GHz) off its bitmap against the axes'
  ticks (a pixel is 6e-4 in $n$ and 4e-6 in $\tan\delta$), where a dash leaves a gap carrying the
  dashes either side to the point. A value is the range over the band, its mean in the notes;
  $\varepsilon' = n^2$, to $\tan^2\delta/4 \approx 10^{-5}$. The paper finds $\tan\delta$ rising
  12–13 % across the band, about as $f^{0.25}$, and three times what the same crystals give at
  10 GHz; a least-squares power law through our reading rises 9.9 % (field along y) and 9.0 %
  (along z) from 91 to 147 GHz. The points scatter by ±0.22e-3 (3.5 %) about it, which leaves
  the fitted rise uncertain by about 3 %, and the paper doesn't say how its 12–13 % was found:
  the difference is noted here, not made a case. Lee's $\varepsilon'$ of 42.5 and 28.2 lie 6.7 %
  below and 7.7 % above the clamped static values in the entry (45.5 and 26.2, Jazbinšek &
  Zgonik); Lee finds his $n$ close to the values reported at 1 and 9 GHz.
- **Silicon.** Krupka et al. measured float-zone p-type silicon as a dielectric resonator and,
  cut smaller, inside a ceramic ring, from 10 to 400 K. At room temperature the loss is the free
  holes' conduction, $\sigma/(\omega\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r)$ (their Eq. (2)), so it falls as
  $1/f$: their 1.866e-3 at 6.685 GHz is about 12 kΩ·cm, and 1.25e-4 at 100 GHz. A handle wafer's
  own resistivity sets its loss. They give $\tan\delta$ to the Q factors' 1–2 % and
  $\varepsilon'$, independent of frequency, to 0.1 %; $\varepsilon'$ isn't printed, and is read
  off their Fig. 2 at 300 K. Lamb's silicon rows keep $f\tan\delta$ within 6 %, a conduction loss
  of about 800 Ω·cm.
- **Lamb's compilation.** Lamb lists each material's values from the papers he cites, without
  judging between them; the catalogue takes his room-temperature rows from 2 to 300 GHz that give
  both $\varepsilon'$ and $\tan\delta$, each with the paper he cites (which isn't read here). The
  loss tangents spread far more than $\varepsilon'$: fused silica's from 1e-4 to 1.8e-3 with the
  glass and its water, crystalline quartz's ordinary from 3.1e-5 to 5.1e-4 between laboratories.
  His fused silica at 393 GHz prints $n$ = 1.9469 beside $\varepsilon'$ = 3.801, whose root is
  1.9496: the same digits with two exchanged, in one or the other, so that row isn't taken.

## Phase-change materials

Sb₂Se₃, Sb₂S₃ and GST (Ge₂Sb₂Te₅) are in the catalogue in both states, each state its own entry
(category `phase-change`: `sb2se3-amorphous`, `sb2se3-crystalline`, …), so a job takes a state as
it takes any material. `catalogue::phase_change("sb2se3")` holds the pair and what switches it:
the crystallization and melting temperatures, the conductivity, the specific heat and the density
($\rho c_p$ by `PhaseChange::heat_capacity`), each a `Constant` with its source; what has no source
yet is in `PhaseChange::missing`.

**Indices.** Delaney et al. 2020 (doi:10.1002/adfm.202002447) measured all six by ellipsometry,
and their data set (doi:10.5258/SOTON/D1247, CC BY 4.0) gives n and k from 194 to 1693 nm. The
catalogue's models are our own least-squares fits to it, the coefficients ours:

| State | Model | Range (µm) | At 1550 nm | Fit within |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sb₂Se₃, amorphous | two-term Sellmeier | 1.0–1.69 | 3.2853 | 8.2e-5 |
| Sb₂Se₃, crystalline | two-term Sellmeier, k from the data at ten wavelengths | 1.201–1.689 | 4.0497 + 2.1e-4 i | 1.5e-5 (n) |
| Sb₂S₃, amorphous | two-term Sellmeier | 1.0–1.69 | 2.7185 | 7.9e-6 |
| Sb₂S₃, crystalline | two-term Sellmeier | 1.0–1.69 | 3.3082 | 4.7e-5 |
| GST, amorphous | two Lorentz oscillators | 1.0–1.69 | 4.352 + 0.061 i | 1.4e-3 (n), 2.3e-3 (k) |
| GST, crystalline | two Lorentz oscillators | 1.0–1.69 | 7.913 + 1.031 i | 1.7e-3 (n), 2.5e-3 (k) |

The fits hold over their ranges only: some terms have negative strengths, standing for the data's
curvature there, not for a resonance. All are room-temperature films (the data set states no
temperature). GST also has Frantz et al. 2023's table (doi:10.1364/OME.506019, through
refractiveindex.info, CC0, every 15th row from 0.99 to 1.71 µm), another film: its crystalline
6.46 + 0.92i at 1550 nm against Delaney's 7.91 + 1.03i.

Against the paper (read in full): Table 1 gives n at 633 to 1550 nm, which the fits meet to its
three decimals at 1060, 1310 and 1550 nm, but for amorphous Sb₂S₃ at 1550 nm, where the table's
2.712 lies 0.0065 below the data set's own 2.7185. Its contrasts, 4.050 − 3.285 = 0.765 and 3.308
− 2.712 = 0.596, are the abstract's 0.77 and 0.60. The data's k is 0 for amorphous Sb₂Se₃ and
both Sb₂S₃ states; crystalline Sb₂Se₃'s is 2.1e-4 at 1550 nm (Table 1 prints 0), above the
abstract's k < 10⁻⁵. The films were 40 nm, sputtered and capped with 200 nm of ZnS:SiO₂.

The paper's figure of merit (its Eq. (1)), `catalogue::figure_of_merit`, is the
phase per dB of loss, $2\pi\Delta n_\mathrm{eff}/(\lambda\alpha)$, with α the loss in the lossier
(crystalline) state. On a 220 nm SOI rib 500 nm wide with a 25 nm film, α is the slope of the insertion
loss against the film's length (Fig. 7a, b): a line through the data set's means gives 0.0096
(Sb₂Se₃) and 0.0288 dB/µm (Sb₂S₃), the paper's 0.01 and 0.028; with its $\Delta n_\mathrm{eff}$ of
0.0717 and 0.0356 the figure is 30.3 and 5.01 rad/dB, its 29.0 and 5.15. The paper doesn't give
the rib's etch depth, how far the film covers it, or the polarization, and neither its data set
nor its Supporting Information does (read in full: Section S3 says only that the simulated guide
is 220 nm SOI with a 25 nm patch and a SiO₂ cap, and its Table S1 gives the modes' overlaps with
the uncoated guide, 0.995 and 0.987 for Sb₂S₃, 0.978 and 0.968 for Sb₂Se₃, 0.956 and 0.342 for
GST, amorphous and crystalline), so its $\Delta n_\mathrm{eff}$ stays not a case. With these fits, the full-vector solver (5 nm cells) gives in TE, for etches of 70,
120, 150 nm and the full 220 nm, 0.019 to 0.032 (Sb₂S₃) and 0.035 to 0.057 (Sb₂Se₃) with the film
on the rib alone, and 0.035 to 0.038 and 0.059 to 0.062 with it over the slab too, against 0.0356
and 0.0717; in TM the two materials move apart further. No etch, coverage and polarization tried
gives both: the one that fits Sb₂S₃ leaves Sb₂Se₃ 13 to 18% short.

A partly crystallized film is the linear effective medium of its two states' permittivities,
`partly_crystalline(amorphous, crystalline, f, λ)`, as Frantz et al. fit GST's intermediate
states (their Section 4.2).

## Tags

The point group is written in both notations, Hermann–Mauguin with overbars and Schoenflies:
silicon $m\bar 3m$ ($O_h$), space group $Fd\bar 3m$; GaAs, AlGaAs, InGaP and InP $\bar 4 3m$
($T_d$), $F\bar 4 3m$; lithium niobate $3m$ ($C_{3v}$), $R3c$; AlN and AlGaN $6mm$ ($C_{6v}$),
$P6_3mc$; barium titanate $4mm$ ($C_{4v}$), $P4mm$; lithium tantalate $3m$, $R3c$; the glasses the limiting group $\infty\infty m$ ($K_h$), on average (J. F. Nye,
*Physical Properties of Crystals*, Oxford, 1957; the International Tables, Vol. A).

**Centrosymmetric (inversion centre)** states a consequence and its limits. Under the inversion
a polar tensor of rank 3 changes sign, $T_{ijk} = -T_{ijk}$, so the bulk $\chi^{(2)}$ and Pockels
$r$ vanish (Neumann's principle). That holds in the electric-dipole approximation: the bulk keeps
weak electric-quadrupole and magnetic-dipole terms; surfaces and interfaces break the inversion;
inhomogeneous strain gives an effective $\chi^{(2)}$ (a few pm/V in simulations of strained
silicon, Sinatkas et al. 2021, p. 010901-12); a static field gives one too,
$\chi^{(2)}_\mathrm{eff} \propto \chi^{(3)} E_0$ (electric-field-induced second harmonics, used
in silicon p–n junctions, Timurdogan et al. 2017); and $\chi^{(3)}$, the Kerr effect, is allowed.

**Non-centrosymmetric** tags state what the point group allows, read from the same table the
tensors follow (`pattern`), so the tags and the tensors can't disagree: $\bar 4 3m$ has
$d_{14} = d_{25} = d_{36}$ and $r_{41} = r_{52} = r_{63}$; $3m$ has $d_{15} = d_{24}$,
$d_{22} = -d_{16} = -d_{21}$, $d_{31} = d_{32}$, $d_{33}$ and $r_{13} = r_{23}$,
$r_{22} = -r_{12} = -r_{61}$, $r_{33}$, $r_{51} = r_{42}$; $6mm$ has $d_{15} = d_{24}$,
$d_{31} = d_{32}$, $d_{33}$ and $r_{13} = r_{23}$, $r_{33}$, $r_{51} = r_{42}$. A test checks that
every value a tensor gives is an element its tag names.

## Conventions

- **Tensors** are in Voigt notation: $d_{il}$ is 3 × 6 with $l = 1 \dots 6$ for
  $jk = 11, 22, 33, 23, 13, 12$, and $r_{ij}$ is 6 × 3 with the same contraction on its first
  index, $\Delta(1/n^2)_i = \sum_k r_{ik} E_k$. Values are in pm/V. $d = \chi^{(2)}/2$, as Roberts
  (1992) recommends and Shoji et al. (1997) follow; a paper quoting $\chi^{(2)}$ is halved.
- **Which elements vanish or are equal comes from the point group**, never from the source:
  $\bar 4 3m$ has $d_{14} = d_{25} = d_{36}$ and $r_{41} = r_{52} = r_{63}$; $3m$ (mirror
  perpendicular to $x$) has $d_{15}, d_{22}, d_{31}, d_{33}$ with $d_{21} = d_{16} = -d_{22}$,
  $d_{24} = d_{15}$, $d_{32} = d_{31}$, and $r_{13}, r_{22}, r_{33}, r_{51}$ with
  $r_{12} = r_{61} = -r_{22}$, $r_{23} = r_{13}$, $r_{42} = r_{51}$; $6mm$ has
  $d_{15} = d_{24}$, $d_{31} = d_{32}$, $d_{33}$ and $r_{13} = r_{23}$, $r_{33}$,
  $r_{42} = r_{51}$. Kleinman's symmetry ($d_{15} = d_{31}$) is not assumed.
- **Clamped and unclamped:** each electro-optic tensor says whether it is $r^S$ (constant strain,
  above the acoustic resonances: what a modulator at GHz sees) or $r^T$ (constant stress, a
  static or low-frequency field), and at which wavelength.
- **Ranges:** a model is valid over the wavelengths, temperatures and compositions its paper
  states, and the library refuses anything outside them. Temperatures are in kelvin.

## Orientation

An anisotropic crystal's permittivity is diagonal in its principal axes, the crystal's x, y and z:
$\mathrm{diag}(n_x^2, n_y^2, n_z^2)$. A uniaxial entry's is $\mathrm{diag}(n_o^2, n_o^2, n_e^2)$,
its optic axis z (the c axis); a biaxial entry (`OpticalClass::Biaxial`, its models giving
`Axis::X`, `Axis::Y` and `Axis::Z`) has $n_x < n_y < n_z$, as Roberts names them.
`Entry::principal` gives the three materials, and `Entry::oriented` turns them onto the chip
(`Material::oriented`, `material::Orientation`).

An orientation's matrix $M$ takes a vector's crystal components to its chip components (chip x
and y in the wafer's plane, z up), and the tensor in the chip's frame is
$\varepsilon = M\,\mathrm{diag}(\varepsilon_x, \varepsilon_y, \varepsilon_z)\,M^\mathsf{T}$
(`Material::tensor`).

- **A cut** names the crystal axis normal to the wafer and the one along the chip's x; the chip's
  y is then z × x, so the frame stays right-handed. An x-cut has the crystal's y along the chip's
  x by default (its c axis along the chip's y: a guide along x is "x-cut, y-propagating", its TE
  mode polarized along c); a y-cut and a z-cut the crystal's x.
- **Euler angles** are proper z-x-z angles $(\varphi, \theta, \psi)$ in degrees, as Goldstein,
  Poole and Safko define them (Classical Mechanics, 3rd ed., 2002, Sec. 4.4; a book, without a
  DOI): $M = R_z(\varphi)\,R_x(\theta)\,R_z(\psi)$, each $R$ the active rotation about that
  axis; their $A$, from the space (chip) frame to the body (crystal) frame, is $M^\mathsf{T}$.
  All zero is the identity, a z-cut; $(0°, 90°, 90°)$ is an x-cut.

In a job, a crystal in a modes or structure job's `[task.stack]` takes an `orientation`:

```yaml
task:
  stack:
    layer:
      - name: LN
        thickness_um: 0.6
        material: { id: linbo3, orientation: x-cut }  # or { cut = "x", along_x = "z" },
        background: sio2                              # or { euler_deg = [0, 90, 90] }
```

The modes job's cross-section takes the tensor in its own axes (across the guide, up, along it).
The full-vector solver's equations (Fallahkhair, Li and Murphy 2008) hold for a tensor with its z
a principal axis, so `job::check` refuses an orientation that puts no principal axis along the
guide; a tilt within the cross-section (an $\varepsilon_{xy}$) is taken. It also refuses an angle
that isn't a number or is beyond ±360°, and an oriented crystal in a static job, whose solver
takes one permittivity per cell. As one number (a picture's), an oriented crystal's
permittivity is the mean of its principal ones, a third of the trace, which no turn changes.

Checked: every cut and Euler angle is a proper rotation, zero angles exactly the identity; a turned
biaxial tensor keeps its trace, principal minors and determinant (its eigenvalues are the
principal values) to 1e-12; Euler $(0°, 90°, 90°)$ is the x-cut's tensor. A 600 nm slab of
$n_o = 2.21$, $n_e = 2.14$ in silica at 1550 nm, guided along x: the x-cut's TE mode is the
isotropic slab of $n_e$ (1.970741) and its TM the one of $n_o$ (1.956465); the z-cut's TE is the
$n_o$ slab's (2.039088) and its TM the uniaxial slab's closed form (1.901142). The full-vector
solver meets each at second order, within 1.1e-5 on a 5 nm grid. A 1 µm × 600 nm LiNbO₃ strip
(Zelmon's congruent model) in silica on a 25 nm grid: the x-cut's TE-like mode 1.85965 and the
z-cut's 1.92533; the same x-cut strip along y, its c axis turned across, has the same indices to
1e-10. The validation report's `material/crystal-*` cases run the turned tensor and the four
slabs at 2.5 nm, each within 3e-6 of its closed form. No published TFLN mode index with its full
geometry has been reproduced yet.

### In FDFD and FDTD

FDFD and FDTD put each component of E on its own point of Yee's grid, so they take a tensor
whose principal axes are the grid's: its diagonal, ε_xx for E_x, ε_yy for E_y, ε_zz for E_z
(`Material::diagonal_permittivity`). Every cut keeps the tensor diagonal in the chip's frame, as
do Euler angles in quarter turns and a uniaxial crystal turned about its optic axis; an
orientation whose tensor couples E's components (an off-diagonal entry beyond 1e-12 of the
diagonal) is refused, with that reason. `Solver2d::diagonal`, `Solver3d::diagonal_on`,
`IterativeSolver3d::diagonal` and `fdtd::Simulation::diagonal` take (ε_xx, ε_yy, ε_zz) at each
point and average each component over its own cell as the scalar problems average ε; the same
three values everywhere is the scalar problem to the bit. `Solver3d::from_structure` takes an
oriented crystal in a stack the same way. A ports' mode is the scheme's own, so it sees the tensor
too. FDTD's update stays scalar, so its Courant limit and stability are a scalar medium's; a tensor
that couples the components is `fdtd::smoothing::Structure`'s, through `Coupling::Triplets`
(stable at any contrast; `Coupling::Nodes` is refused where it isn't positive definite, #209).

In jobs: an `"fdtd"` job in 3D gives each component its entry, its bodies' crystals too, and
solves its mode sources and monitors with the tensor; a plane wave or a beam must start in an
isotropic medium. A 2D job (`"fdfd"`, or `"fdtd"` in 2D) is the layer's plane by the effective
index method. Its TE plane has E in the plane, and the slab's TE mode sees only the ε of the
component it is polarized along, so E_x's effective index is the slab's of each medium's ε_xx
and E_y's of its ε_yy (one plane when every medium has ε_xx = ε_yy, as a z-cut); averaging from
the fill takes one plane, so it refuses a crystal with ε_xx ≠ ε_yy. The slab's TM mode sees
ε_zz and the in-plane ε along an unknown direction of travel, and the slab's solver is
isotropic: a 2D TM job refuses an oriented crystal.

Checked (`fdfd/crystal-*`, `fdtd/crystal-turned`): the four slabs above as 2D FDFD port modes on
the slab's vertical cross-section, at 2.5 nm within 3.4e-5 to 4.0e-5 of their closed forms, at
second order (z-cut TM: 5.4e-4, 1.4e-4, 3.4e-5 at 10, 5 and 2.5 nm, order 2.000); and an x-cut
crystal beside a source in 2D FDFD, 3D FDFD from a stack and 3D FDTD, against the problem
mirrored across x = y with the crystal turned a quarter turn about z: the same field, mirrored,
to 2.8e-15, 7.5e-15 and 2.7e-16 of its largest. In an `"fdfd"` job a 1 µm LiNbO₃ rib, 300 nm in
oxide, on a 40 nm grid: the x-cut's TE port index 1.6928 (E_y on the c axis, n_e), the z-cut's
1.7448 (n_o). A published TFLN result with its full geometry in FDFD or FDTD isn't reproduced yet.

No biaxial crystal is in the catalogue yet. KTP from Kato and Takaoka (Appl. Opt. 41, 5040
(2002), [doi:10.1364/AO.41.005040](https://doi.org/10.1364/AO.41.005040)) is the first wanted,
read from the paper itself, which isn't in hand.
